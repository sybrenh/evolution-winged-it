Ten animals, fixed with whatever was lying around

EnglishNederlands

Nature's finest designs workarounds

Evolution can't send anything back to the drawing board. Each generation inherits the last one's body and can only adjust it, so living things are full of patches, detours and parts doing jobs they were never meant for. Stephen Jay Gould wrote 300 monthly essays arguing that these oddities are the best evidence for evolution there is. Here are ten, several straight from his pages.

Bottlenose dolphin Tursiops truncatus

Left the sea, grew legs, changed its mind

Some 375 million years ago, fish hauled themselves onto land. Their descendants grew legs, lungs and fur, and in time became mammals. Then, about 50 million years ago, one hoofed mammal started wading back in.

Its descendants are the whales and dolphins, and they kept everything land had given them. They breathe air, so they must surface every few minutes for life. They nurse their young underwater. Their nostrils crept to the top of the head and became a blowhole. Their closest living relatives are hippos.

The giveaway

Fish swing their tails from side to side. Dolphins beat theirs up and down, because their spine still bends the way a running mammal's does. A swimming dolphin is galloping.

Gould, “Hooking Leviathan by Its Past”, in Dinosaur in a Haystack (1995)

Giant panda Ailuropoda melanoleuca

The bear that built a thumb out of its wrist

Pandas are bears, and bears are built to eat meat. Pandas eat bamboo anyway, for up to 14 hours a day, with a carnivore's gut that digests less than a fifth of it.

Stripping bamboo takes a thumb. A bear's real thumb was lined up with the other four fingers long ago, fine for clawing and useless for gripping. So evolution enlarged a small wrist bone, the radial sesamoid, until it stuck out far enough to pinch a stalk against. The muscles that work it were rerouted from the real thumb next door.

Gould made this the title essay of his best-known book. No engineer would build it, he wrote. A tinkerer with nothing but a bear to work with would.

The giveaway

The false thumb has no joints and no claw. It is one lump of bone under a pad, which makes the panda the only bear that appears to have six fingers.

Gould, “The Panda's Thumb”, in The Panda's Thumb (1980)

Chicken Gallus gallus & horse Equus caballus

Chickens still know how to grow teeth

Birds lost their teeth roughly 100 million years ago. The instructions were never fully deleted. In 1980 researchers paired chick jaw tissue with tissue from a mouse embryo, and the graft grew teeth. In 2006 a mutant chicken embryo turned up with rows of cone-shaped tooth buds, like a baby crocodile's.

Horses tell the same story from the other end. Their ancestors trotted on three or four toes per foot. A modern horse stands on one. Now and then a foal is born with an extra toe or two, small hooves and all. Julius Caesar is said to have ridden such a horse.

For Gould, throwbacks like these showed that the past stays on file. Old genes are rarely erased. They are switched off, and a switch can slip.

The giveaway

A horse's front “knee” is its wrist. It runs on the tip of one middle finger, and the hoof is the fingernail.

Gould, “Hen's Teeth and Horse's Toes”, in Hen's Teeth and Horse's Toes (1983)

Greater flamingo Phoenicopterus roseus

Eats upside down, so its face was rebuilt to match

A flamingo feeds with its head upside down, bill pointing back toward its feet, sieving shrimp and algae out of the mud. It has done this for so long that its jaws have traded places.

In nearly every other bird the upper bill is the big half and the lower jaw is the small one that moves. In a flamingo the upper bill is a thin lid and the lower is a deep trough. Stand the bird upright and its bill looks as if it was fitted the wrong way up. Turn the head over, the way the flamingo actually uses it, and everything is where you'd expect: big half on top, small half working below.

The giveaway

The tongue is the pump. It drives water through the bill's comb-like filters several times a second. Roman emperors served it as a delicacy.

Gould, “The Flamingo's Smile”, in The Flamingo's Smile (1985)

North Island brown kiwi Apteryx mantelli

A chicken-sized bird with an egg six times too big

A kiwi is about the size of a chicken. Its egg weighs close to half a kilo, around six times what a bird that size should lay, and can make up a fifth of the mother's weight. In the last days before laying there is almost no room left inside her for food.

Gould's explanation was that the kiwi is a shrunken giant. Its ancestors were big birds like the moa, he argued, and as the body shrank over the generations the egg shrank more slowly. The bird got smaller and the egg never caught up.

DNA has since complicated things. A 2014 study found the kiwi's closest relatives were the elephant birds of Madagascar, and that its ancestors probably flew to New Zealand, so they may never have been giants at all. The question is open again. The egg is still enormous.

The giveaway

The chick hatches fully feathered with its eyes open, carrying enough yolk in its belly to skip its first days of meals. Its parents never feed it.

Gould, “Of Kiwi Eggs and the Liberty Bell”, in Bully for Brontosaurus (1991)

Giraffe Giraffa camelopardalis

The nerve that takes a four-metre detour

The nerve that works a giraffe's voice box leaves the brain a short distance from where it needs to go. It doesn't go there. It runs all the way down the neck into the chest, loops under a main artery beside the heart, and climbs all the way back up. In a tall giraffe that is a round trip of more than four metres.

The route made sense in fish. They have no neck, and the nerve ran straight past the heart to the gills. Then necks evolved and the heart settled deep in the chest, with the nerve still hooked underneath that artery. Each generation could stretch the nerve a little further. None could unhook it.

The giveaway

You have the same loop in a shorter edition. The long-necked sauropod dinosaurs had it far worse: by one estimate, about 28 metres of nerve.

Richard Dawkins, The Greatest Show on Earth (2009)

Deep-sea anglerfish suborder Ceratioidei

The husband who became an organ

A kilometre or two down, in permanent darkness, finding a mate is close to impossible. Some deep-sea anglerfish solved the problem by making sure that a male who finds a female never leaves.

The male is tiny, in some species under a centimetre long, with oversized nostrils for following her scent. When he finds her he bites her side and holds on. His mouth fuses with her skin and their bloodstreams join. His eyes and most of his organs waste away until he is little more than a pair of testes attached to her body, fed by her blood. A female can carry several.

The biologist who first described a female with males attached took the little lumps for her babies.

The giveaway

To make the merger work, these species have thrown out parts of their own immune system: the parts that would reject a transplant.

Gould, “Big Fish, Little Fish”, in Hen's Teeth and Horse's Toes (1983)

European plaice Pleuronectes platessa

Lay down on its side, then had to move an eye

A baby plaice looks like any other fish: upright, symmetrical, one eye on each side. A few weeks in, its left eye starts to drift. It travels up over the top of the head and settles beside the right one, and the skull twists to make room. Then the fish lies down on its blind side and stays there for life.

Rays flattened the sensible way, belly down. The ancestors of flatfish were tall, narrow fish that could only lie flat by tipping over, which left one eye staring into the sand. Evolution moved the eye and kept the rest of the fish.

The giveaway

Fossils about 50 million years old show the halfway stage: adult fish with one eye stranded near the top of the head, not yet across.

Matt Friedman, “The evolutionary origin of flatfish asymmetry”, Nature (2008)

Red king crab Paralithodes camtschaticus

Evolution keeps inventing the crab

A wide, flat shell. A tail tucked away underneath. Legs out to the sides. This body has evolved from scratch at least five separate times, in crustaceans that started out shaped more like lobsters or hermit crabs. Biologists have a word for it: carcinisation, the process of becoming a crab.

So a king crab is no true crab. It descends from hermit crabs that gave up their borrowed shells and grew armour of their own. Nobody is sure why the crab shape is such a reliable destination, and a few lineages have since evolved back out of it.

The giveaway

Count the walking legs. True crabs show four pairs. King crabs show three, because the fourth pair is tiny and folded away inside the shell, where it cleans the gills.

Keiler, Wirkner & Richter, “One hundred years of carcinization”, Biological Journal of the Linnean Society (2017)

Human Homo sapiens

And then there's you

You are a rebuilt fish as well, and the alterations show. Six places to look.

  • A hole in your vision

    Your retina is installed backwards. Its wiring runs across the front of the light sensors and leaves through a hole, where you see nothing. Octopus eyes evolved separately, with the wiring behind, and have no blind spot.

    +

    Cover your left eye. Stare at the cross and move slowly toward the screen. The dot will vanish.

  • Hiccups

    One hypothesis traces them to tadpoles, which gulp water over their gills with the same sharp intake and snap of the throat. The nerve that triggers the spasm also takes a long, exposed route down from the neck, a leftover from when the diaphragm's ancestors sat near the gills.

  • Goosebumps

    When you are cold or frightened, a tiny muscle pulls each hair upright. This fluffs up a fur coat you no longer own.

  • A tailbone

    At about five weeks, every human embryo has a tail. Over the next few weeks it is absorbed again, leaving a few fused bones at the base of the spine that you only notice when you fall on them.

  • Nipples on men

    Embryos lay down nipples before the differences between the sexes switch on. Gould used them to argue that not every body part needs a purpose. Some are simply the cost of building two sexes from one plan.

  • Choking

    Lungs began as a pouch off the gut of a fish, so your air and your food still share a crossing in the throat. Every swallow is a small act of traffic control.

Gould, “Male Nipples and Clitoral Ripples”, in Bully for Brontosaurus (1991). The hiccup hypothesis is told in Neil Shubin, Your Inner Fish (2008)

Odd arrangements and funny solutions are the proof of evolution

Stephen Jay Gould, The Panda's Thumb, 1980

A perfect body tells you nothing about where it came from. A thumb made from a wrist, a tail that gallops and an egg that barely fits each carry a record of what the animal used to be. That was Gould's argument, made month after month in his Natural History column from 1974 to 2001 without missing an issue.
